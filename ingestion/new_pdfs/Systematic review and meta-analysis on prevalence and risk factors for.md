## ABSTRACT

**Objectives:** Gingival recession is a common mucogingival condition. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to assess the prevalence of gingival recession and identify associated risk factors in the general population.

**Study selection:** Observational studies reporting prevalence and risk factors for gingival recession published since 2000 were included. Methodological quality was assessed using the modified Newcastle-Ottawa scale for cross-sectional studies. Random-effect meta-analyses were conducted for the prevalence (%) of gingival recession at different cut-off scores (≥1 mm, ≥3 mm, and ≥5 mm) and odds ratios (OR) of identified risk factors.

**Sources:** MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science were systematically searched in November 2024. Additionally, a hand search was performed. The study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024516816).

**Data:** 21 sources, reporting on 22 studies were included in this systematic review. Most of the included studies represented a low risk of bias. Overall, estimated prevalence of gingival recession was 81.1 % (95 %-CI: 73.9–86.7) for ≥1 mm, 48.4 % (95 %-CI: 39.7–57.2) for ≥3 mm, and 16.2 % (95 %-CI: 9.1–27.4) for ≥5 mm. Risk factors were structured into domains. Meta-analyses revealed male gender (p<sub>adj.</sub> <0.001; OR=1.52, 95 %-CI: 1.36–1.69), smoking (p<sub>adj.</sub>=0.003; OR=1.84, 95 %-CI: 1.33–2.53), alcohol consumption (p<sub>adj.</sub> <0.001; OR=2.04, 95 %-CI: 1.51–2.75), dental plaque (p<sub>adj.</sub> <0.001; OR=4.26, 95 %-CI: 2.91–6.24), presence of a high frenulum (p<sub>adj.</sub> <0.001; OR=4.58, 95 %-CI: 2.58–8.11), occlusal trauma (p<sub>adj.</sub>=0.003; OR=3.20, 95 %-CI: 1.74–5.87), periodontitis (p<sub>adj.</sub> <0.001; OR=9.90, 95 %-CI: 4.15–23.60), and history of periodontal treatment (p<sub>adj.</sub> <0.001; OR=1.86, 95 %-CI: 1.33–2.58) to be significantly associated with gingival recession.

**Conclusions:** Observational studies indicated that gingival recession is a highly prevalent condition associated with a variety of risk factors.

**Clinical significance:** Gingival recession is associated with periodontal conditions like periodontitis, high frenulum, and modifiable factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and occlusal trauma. Clinicians should primarily focus on identifying these risk factors and implementing preventive strategies.

## 1. Introduction

Gingival recession is a common mucogingival condition that affects many patients. It is defined as the exposure of the root surface due to an apical shift of the gingival margin beyond the cemento-enamel junction [1]. This condition is associated with clinical attachment loss caused by different factors, which may be localized or generalized [1,2]. A variety of potential risk factors, e.g. socio-demographics, tooth malposition, phenotype, mechanical trauma, plaque-induced inflammation, and history of orthodontic treatment, might lead to an apical shift of the gingiva [2,3]. In addition to aesthetic and functional complaints [4], gingival recession may increase the risk of root caries [5,6], non-carious cervical lesions [5], and patients’ subjective fear of tooth loss [7]. Gingival recession is a health issue with a psychosocial impact on

---

patients [8,9]. However, these aspects are less in focus of evidence-based epidemiology and periodontal research.

The prevalence of gingival recession in general population is high [3]. So far, one systematic review and meta-analysis by Yadav et al. [10] found a global prevalence of about 85 percent. Previous observational studies demonstrated a multifactorial etiology of gingival recession [11–14]. Regarding risk factors for gingival recession, only one systematic review analyzed the association between potential risk factors and gingival recession after orthodontic treatment [15]. Unfortunately, there are no systematic reviews and meta-analyses that focus on the general population. The high psychosocial impact of gingival recession on patients, including aesthetic and functional complaints, makes it necessary to identify patients at risk and to assess potentially related risk factors to prevent gingival recessions at an early stage. Prevention strategies for gingival recession are underestimated in periodontal research as researchers have primarily focused on surgical techniques and biomaterials for root coverage [16–18].

This study aimed to identify the prevalence in the general population and to map potential risk factors and their association with gingival recession in observational (cross-sectional) studies. Studies published from 2000 onward were included to ensure relevant data on the etiology of gingival recession and reported risk factors.

# 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Protocol

This systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024516816) prior to its initiation and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19].

## 2.2. Research question

The study aimed to determine the prevalence of gingival recession in the permanent dentition and to identify potential risk factors associated with it in the general population since the year 2000.

## 2.3. Eligibility criteria

Following the PECOS (population, exposure, control, outcome, and study design) statement, the eligibility criteria included a general population of individuals of all ages and gender with permanent dentition (P). The exposure criteria were defined as the presence of gingival recession, clinically measured by a validated method (e.g. Miller's classification [20], Cairo's classification $\ge$1 [21]) and/or measured by a defined cut-off score ($\ge$1 mm, $\ge$3 mm, $\ge$5 mm) (E), while the control was defined as individuals without gingival recession in the permanent dentition (C). The outcome included prevalence and evaluation of risk factors significantly associated with gingival recession (O). Observational studies published in English or German since 2000 were included in this systematic review (S).

Exclusion criteria included studies that involved a specific subset of the general population (e.g. gender-specific, such as only males or females, students, specific migrant populations, and individuals with specific diseases or syndromes), those with insufficient data, case reports, review articles, studies reporting only prevalence, and those lacking relevance to risk factors. Additionally, studies without full-mouth examination were also excluded.

## 2.4. Search strategy

In November 2024 a systematic literature search was performed in four different electronic databases (MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase) using the following search term ("gingival recession" AND ("risk factors" OR "risk indicators" OR "risk assessment" OR etiology OR epidemiology OR prevalence NOT implant* NOT therapy)). In addition, a manual search using Google and Google Scholar (for grey literature) was performed, and the reference lists of included sources were screened. The search term was slightly adjusted to meet the specific requirements of each electronic database. The complete search terms for all electronic databases are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

## 2.5. Study selection

After the initial search, duplicate records were identified and removed using a reference manager (EndNote 21.2, www.endnote.com). The titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (F.M. and C.L.), who proceeded to a full-text evaluation of potentially relevant sources. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion. The reasons for excluding sources after the full-text review are detailed in Supplemental Table S2. In case of missing data, authors were contacted via email. After 2 weeks, a second reminder was sent to non-responders. If authors did not respond or if the required information could not be provided, the studies were excluded from the respective meta-analysis.

## 2.6. Data extraction

The data extraction from the included studies was independently carried out by two reviewers (F.M. and C.L.) using a pre-tested spreadsheet. If available, extracted information included study characteristics (i.e. authors, year of publication, country, continent, recruitment and sampling method), number of participants, age range, inclusion and exclusion criteria, reference level and minimum cut-off for assessment of gingival recession, prevalence (at the following cut-off scores: $\ge$1 mm, $\ge$3 mm, $\ge$5 mm), reported risk factors, and results of the statistical analysis (significant vs. non-significant risk factors vs. not reported) for each assessed risk factor. Risk factors for gingival recession were categorized into following seven domains: socio-demographics, socio-economics, general health conditions, general health behaviors, dental health behaviors, dental health conditions, and periodontal health conditions. To enable quantitative synthesis, the absolute numbers of participants with and without gingival recession were extracted for each reported risk factor. In case of studies reporting on multiple cut-off scores, the proportion of patients with gingival recession was based on the highest cut-off score.

## 2.7. Data synthesis and meta-analyses

To determine the prevalence (%) of gingival recession, meta-analyses were performed for the following cut-off scores, which were the most commonly reported: $\ge$1 mm, $\ge$3 mm, and $\ge$5 mm. Additionally, multivariable meta-regression analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between the prevalence of gingival recession, geographic location of study (continents), and the year of publication.

Identified risk factors and their respective significance in each study were visually presented in a heatmap, similar to the approach used in previous studies [22,23]. Additionally, meta-analyses of odds ratios (ORs) were conducted for each risk factor domain. Within each domain, individual risk factors reported uniformly as dichotomous variables were categorized into subgroups, and pooled-effect estimates were calculated for each subgroup.

Statistical analysis was conducted using the software R (www.r-project.org, version 4.4.1) along with the packages "meta" (version 8.0–1) and "metafor" (version 4.6–0). Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q test and $I^2$ statistics [24]. Due to high heterogeneity being present throughout all analyses (p-values of Cochran's Q test < 0.05, $I^2$ values $\ge$50 %), random effects meta-analyses were performed. To assess publication bias, Funnel Plots and Egger's regression intercept test were used [25]. The significance level was set at

---

$\alpha = 0.05$. Given that multiple meta-analyses and subgroups were assessed, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing according to Bonferroni-Holm.

## 2.8. Quality assessment

Included studies were assessed to the methodological quality by two independent reviewers (F.M. and C.L.) using the modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for cross-sectional studies [26,27]. Each study was evaluated using a star (*) rating system, which assessed three components: selection (up to 5 stars), comparability (up to 2 stars), and outcome (up to 3 stars). A total of 9 to 10 stars indicated high quality, 7 to 8 stars indicated good quality, 5 to 6 stars indicated satisfactory quality, and 0 to 4 stars indicated unsatisfactory quality [26,27]. For further details see Supplemental Table S3.

## 2.9. Evidence assessment

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to assess the quality of the evidence [28]. For up- or downgrading the evidence of the findings, the evaluation criteria considered factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

# 3. Results

## 3.1. Study characteristics

A total of 2621 potentially relevant records were considered for inclusion. Following the removal of duplicates and the exclusion of records based on title and/or abstract, only 43 sources were selected for full-text examination. After full-text reading, 24 sources were excluded (Supplemental Table S2). A manual search identified 3 publications. Finally, 21 sources [11–14,29–46] reporting on 22 studies were included in this systematic review. Further details regarding the literature search are presented in the PRISMA flowchart in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of the included sources are presented in Table 1. A total of 37,263 participants (range: 104 [41] to 10,676 [14] participants per study) with an age range of 14 to 103 years were included. Most of studies were conducted in Europe (38.1 % of the studies) [11,13,30,34,36,39–41], followed by Asia (28.6 % of the studies) [29,32,35,38,44,46], and South America (23.8 % of the studies) [12,31,33,37,42,43]. One study was conducted in Africa [45], while another was conducted in  North America [14].

## 3.2. Quality assessment of included sources

The results of the risk of bias assessment for each included source are summarized in Supplemental Table S3. Half of the included sources received a good quality score (11/22), while 10 included sources were considered to have a high quality. Only one source received a satisfactory quality.

## 3.3. Prevalence of gingival recession

Twenty sources were included in the meta-analysis of the prevalence of gingival recession at the cut-off score of $\ge$1 mm [11–14,29–35,37–40,42–46], while 11 sources were included in the meta-analysis based on a cut-off at $\ge$3 mm [11–14,31,32,36,37,40,42–44], and 7 sources in the meta-analysis using a cut-off score of $\ge$5 mm [11,14,31,32,37,40,42,43]. The overall estimated prevalence of gingival recession in the permanent dentition in the general population was stratified by cut-off scores, with values of 81.1 % (95 %-CI: 73.9–86.7) for $\ge$1 mm (Fig. 2), 48.4 % (95 %-CI: 39.7–57.2) for $\ge$3 mm (Fig. 3), and 16.2 % (95 %-CI: 9.1–27.4) for $\ge$5 mm (Fig. 4). There was no indication of relevant publication bias based on the statistical and graphical analyses (Supplemental Figure S1).

Meta-regression showed a significant impact of geographic location on the prevalence of gingival recession at all cut-off scores. For a cut-off score of $\ge$1 mm, studies from South America reported a significantly higher prevalence compared to Europe ($p = 0.032$). For $\ge$3 mm, studies from Asia showed a significantly lower prevalence ($p = 0.002$). At a cut-off score of $\ge$5 mm, both North America and South America exhibited significantly higher prevalence rates than Europe ($p < 0.001$). Study sample size had an impact at a cut-off score of $\ge$5 mm, with larger studies reporting lower prevalence rates ($p < 0.001$). For further details see Table 2.

## 3.4. Risk factors for gingival recession

In total, 46 potential risk factors for gingival recession were reported in the included studies. Their association with gingival recession was graphically documented in a heat map (Fig. 5). Risk factors were structured into seven domains: socio-demographics, socio-economics, general health conditions, general health behaviors, dental health behaviors, dental health conditions, and periodontal health conditions.

### 3.5. Assessment of evidence

The quality of evidence from the meta-analyses related to the prevalence of gingival recession in the general population and domains of risk factors is presented in Table 4. According to GRADE recommendations, the overall evidence has been downgraded to a very low level of evidence, due to the reliance on observational studies, inconsistency (i.e. high heterogeneity), and imprecision in the reported estimates (Supplemental Figures S2-S7). Relevant publication bias based on Funnel Plots and results of Egger’s regression intercept test (Supplemental Figures S1 and S8) was only found in one domain (i.e. periodontal health conditions).

## 4. Discussion

The overall estimated prevalence of gingival recession amounted to 81.1 % when a cut-off score of ≥1 mm was applied. This is in the range of the 84.9 % global pooled prevalence reported in a recent meta-analysis based on the same cut-off score [10]. This finding indicates a highly

prevalent condition in the general population, which could reflect age-related changes [5]. However, it is important to not overestimate the clinical relevance of mild gingival recessions (≥1 mm), as several studies have shown that most of these cases are asymptomatic and do not result in aesthetic or functional impairments [47–49]. For higher cut-off scores (≥3 mm and ≥5 mm), we found that the overall estimated prevalence was 48.4 % and 16.2 %, respectively.

Only population-based studies reporting the prevalence of gingival recession and potential risk factors were included in this study. In accordance to Yadav et al. [10], meta-regression analysis revealed no significant effect of the year of publication, indicating no increase or decrease in the global prevalence of gingival recession since 2000. In contrast, the geographic location exhibited a significant influence for all three cut-off scores. For a cut-off score of ≥1 mm, South America reported a higher prevalence compared to Europe. At a cut-off score of ≥3 mm, Asia demonstrated a lower prevalence, while at ≥5 mm both North America and South America showed significantly higher prevalence rates for gingival recession relative to Europe. This might be based on a lower prevalence of periodontal disease in countries with better access to dental care than in regions with limited access [50]. This systematic review revealed a total of 46 potential risk factors for gingival recession that were assessed in observational studies. Socio-demographic factors like age and gender, and dental health conditions and periodontal health conditions were most often, but not consistently, assessed. Meta-analyses of potential risk factors were performed for dichotomous risk factors reported in a uniform way (e.g. consistent cut-offs and/or groups). As main limitation of the present study several other variables (e.g. age, bleeding on probing) could not be included due to their inconsistent reporting and lack of raw datasets. Unfortunately, meta-analysis for the domain of general health conditions was also not possible due to a lack of sufficient data. Consequently, the domains general health conditions, general health behaviors, and dental health conditions are underrepresented in our meta-analyses and need to be further assessed in future studies. On the other hand, methodological strengths of this systematic review include a systematic search across four databases, the application of the GRADE framework to assess the quality of evidence for each outcome (prevalence and domains), and the systematic mapping of nearly all potential risk factors for gingival recession reported in observational studies conducted since 2000.

These results are consistent with the findings of recent studies, which identify male gender, smoking, and periodontitis as key risk factors for gingival recession [10,51–53]. Periodontal conditions are highly prevalent in males [54,55] who also tend to exhibit less favorable health behaviors such as poor oral hygiene [55], higher rates of smoking [55,56], and alcohol consumption [55,57]. These behavioral patterns likely explain the observed association between male gender and gingival recession, which can be attributed to the multifactorial etiological causes of periodontal diseases that contribute to clinical attachment loss and the progression of circular gingival recessions [58]. It is important to consider that these risk factors may interact with each other and potentially confound the results.

Furthermore, smoking is a well-established risk factor for gingival recession [3,10]. Patients should be supported through structured smoking cessation programs, including both individual counseling sessions and group programs [59,60], as well as the use of nicotine replacement therapies [61,62]. Alongside smoking cessation, alcohol cessation should also be a key focus of patient education, as alcohol consumption disrupts the balance of the oral microbiota and promotes inflammation [63].

We found that male gender, general health behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption, dental plaque, the presence of a high frenulum, occlusal trauma, periodontitis, and a history of periodontal treatment are significantly associated with gingival recession. Also, dental plaque has been identified as a significant factor in the development of gingival recession in several clinical studies [51,52], closely associated with attachment loss. # 5. Conclusions

Based on the assessment of the global prevalence of gingival recession, it is evident that this condition is highly common. Various risk factors (e.g. gender, oral health behaviors, and periodontal conditions) were identified as associated with gingival recession. It is essential for clinicians to inform patients about these risk factors to improve oral health behavior.
